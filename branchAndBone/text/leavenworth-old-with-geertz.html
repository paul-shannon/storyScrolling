Fort Leavenworth, Kansas

This is where I lived. This is where I rode my bike out from the day I discovered the forest. As best I reconstruct, our quarters were on the second floor of the three-story building in the lower left of the photo. You can see a few trees, many automobiles, a brutalist darker building towards the top. Everything is shaped by human hands to fulfill utilitarian (military) purposes.

The forest and the fort: each had a metaphysics — by which I mean a system describing the objects of which they were constructed, and their importance, and the power relations among them. Since this system was not worked out formally and written up, as a metaphysical system would be in academic philosophy, let's call each a "folk metaphysics", an informal sense of each world, how it was constituted, and the rules by which it worked. Some alternative terms for this: worldview, an imaginary, ideology, umwelt, gestalt, a Foucauldian episteme.

We acquire these from our culture, from our parents, with likely some alterations as we live our life. They function as a cognitive lens, an image of the world through which we then view the world, bringing some things and relations into focus, leaving other things and other relations blurry, or not visible at all. They are rarely pure. Most of us have a few; the come into and out of play depending on circumstance. A devout Catholic army officer may inhabit one metaphysics on Sundays, quite another during the week.

Another angle on this notion comes from the crucial late 20th century symbolic anthropologist Clifford Geertz:

Believing, with Max Weber, that man is an animal suspended in webs of significance he himself has spun, I take culture to be those webs, and the analysis of it to be therefore not an experimental science in search of law but an interpretive one in search of meaning.

Geertz's metaphor — webs of significance — suggests that one's metaphysics (I'll stick with that term for now) consists of connected threads, some of which might be stories, each individually strong (a spider's silken thread has the tensile strength of steel) and joined reliably togehter.

Yet some spiders reweave their webs. Others rebuild them completely.

My task, going on now for 65 years, has been not exactly to reweave or completely rebuild the webs of significance into which I was born, but instead, to develop two webs, side-by-side, as best I can, one for the green world, one for the modern human world in which I necessarily also live. I suspect that a well-worked out web of the green world - my goal in this project - will have some lessons for the modern human world. Hence my promise in the title with which I began, "Branch and Bone: lessons from a small forest".
