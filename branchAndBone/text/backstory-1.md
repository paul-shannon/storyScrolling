### A crummy first draft: Living in Two Worlds


The first of my two worlds was human-built and human-dominated, to
which I was introduced by my parents.  This was in 1950s America, in
the "glorious thirty years" following World War II.  We were a family
of four. I was the younger, by sixteen months, of two sons.  My father
was a handsome Army officer, a West Point graduate, then rising in the
ranks of cold war missile programs.  My mother was a UC Berkely
graduate, previously a a glamorous airline stewardess.  Both parents
had extracted themselves from their own birth families; due to that,
and because we constantly moved around, we had no extended family to
speak of, I knew no cousins, aunts or uncles.  And very significant
for me, I had a frequently severe stutter, bad enough to complicate my
every day, which brought me isolation, anxiety and struggle.  I showed
some early signs of mathematical skill.  I had early on developed a
love of stories and books.

As for the other world: my first encounter came at the age of seven, when I
rode my bike alone to the edge of our US Army post.  I had no
destination in mind, no map to guide me.  I happened upon a low bridge
over a wide sun-dappled stream surrounded by tall trees.  There was
nobody around, there was no awkward speaking situation to navigate or
to try to avoid.  There was beauty all around, beauty such as I had
never seen, heard about or imagined.  

I have lived my life in all of the years since in these two worlds.
My participation in that first world, the world of human relations,
of history an politics and culture: I had little choice but to join
in. (My speech impediment, so painful to me when young, softened in my
early 30s, allowing me an eaier fit into this first world.)

The second world, a green world of forest, of free-flowing water and
sunlight - since that day, that bike ride, this world has always
beckoned me.

How does one live in two such different worlds, how navigate between
them?  I use two strategies.  To put it simply, overly simply: I grew
up both a science boy and a nature boy.  Until retiring a couple of
years ago, I did research into the molecular biology of human disease,
creating interactive computer visualizations of processes and
structures otherwise difficult to see.  And I have spent thousands of
hours, months of days, walking in mountains and studying (sometimes
protecting) old-growth forests.  All along, probably with the same
source as that which led me to be a science boy, I am always curious
to know, and to ask "how does that work?"

The trick is to ask the question - to take on the posture of the
analyst standing apart from the object of study - without losing the
experience of being beckoned into, being welcomed by, being part of
the green world.

A distinction between knowledge and understanding helps with this
trick.   "Knowledge" consists of certain truths, verified, often
rigorously mathematical, consisting of or making use of physical laws,
which describe relationships which are universally true and
exceptionless.  (see Sandra Mitchell, Unsimple Truths, about 31%).

Understanding (see Catherine Elgin) can be very different.  But not
less useful, especially when trying to comprehend unsimple situations,
which consist of many different parts influenced by their prior
history.  More useful here are exemplification and metaphor,
highlighting the similarity between a not-yet-comprehended target and
a partially similar yet better understood reference.
