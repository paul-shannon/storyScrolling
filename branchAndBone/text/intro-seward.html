There have been many forests for me since then — decades walking in the Blue Ridge Mountains of Virginia and on old dirt roads through the mountains of West Virgina then, for the last thirty years, a small forest in Seattle, on a peninsula, in a city park.

This is old-growth, a full 500 years since the last stand-replacement fire, a rare remnant of the million acres of forest once found here in the Puget Lowlands, between the Cascade Mountains to the east, and the Olympic Mountains to the west.

Giant Douglas-firs, tiny Douglas squirrels, pileated woodpeckers and crowned kinglets, hemlock and cedar, eagle and owl.

This old forest is where I go — somtimes, as when I was a kid, by bike. I do my best to take in its complex beauty, try to learn some of the secrets of its green world.

Some of that elegance and some of those secrets are described below. A mix of disciplines and strategies help out: natural history, molecular biology, geology, forest ecology, fire history. But not least: just looking. On a rare day walking here, with all those strategies engaged, the analytic aspects fall away and I find myself but another "true creature of no rank" of this old endlessly creative planet, my bones not so different from tree branches, my breathing interleaved with and depending upon sunlight capture by leaves, my cognition just another version of all the sensing and deciding going on all around me in the other creatures of this green world, all of us made from some combination of these things:

sunlight and water
leaf and lung
branch and bone
