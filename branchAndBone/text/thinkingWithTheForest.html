Thinking with an old-growth forest

We humans tend to think with, and to use metaphors inspired by the compelling items in our environment, especially when novel - surprising - and powerful. They excite analogies which sometimes then diffuse through the culture, picked up as explanatory strategies in other domains. Our imaginations are shaped by the compelling objects, forces, processes that surround us. This is, to adopt the term, the imaginary in which we live. It is various, it is historical, yet trends over time can be seen.

Trees and forests in urban life are rarely surprising, powerful or compelling.

But Seward Park's urban old-growth forest can be all three.

Here are a few examples of this phenomenon. I suggest we should add this old-growth forest to the list.

  • Aristotle: his close study of the animals in bay of Kallonis on the Greek island of Lesbos, shaped the "biological" metaphysics for which he is known.
  • Darwin: Lyell's uniformitarianism, the lands visited during his five years sailing on the Beagle, Malthus on population all influenced his evolutionary ideas.
  • Freud: the energy flows and regulation in the steam engine, along with 19th century advances in hydraulics, offered models for the human mind.
  • Cogntive psychology: brains were modeled after computers.

In the last 500 years, with Luther's and Calvin's Reformation, the rise of early capitalism, the nation state, and the growing presence of machines in our lives, we have new imaginaries to think with.

Especially for urban citizens of techological cultures, who live in an almost entirely human-built world, with straight lines everywhere - the carpentered environment - often, too often perhaps, where the primary forces and agents of change are human. One common imaginary of the present day is organized around buying and selling, humans as customers, everything a commodity best understood in terms of its value in the market. Something special is attributed to humans; all other species are ancillary, surbordinate, of only passing interest.

But just down the hill, a fifteen minute walk, lies quite a different "compelling item", one that draws my attention, and which I use to think with. It provides metaphors which guide me, models to reason with: 120 acres of Douglas-Fir old-growth forest. In what follows I explore this forest and I explore the imaginary it has shaped in me.
