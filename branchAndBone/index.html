Branch and Bone

Lessons from a small forest

I encountered my first forest at age seven while riding my bike on the military base where I then lived. I came upon a sun-dappled stream running beneath a low bridge. There were no other people around, only tall trees understory and the stream. I had of course seen trees before and surely a river or two. But never an entire interwoven green world (as it then seemed to me) — growing on its own terms with such beauty. In which I felt at home.

That early encounter posed a question I have been working on ever since. What is the relationship between this green world, of sunlight and water, of leaf and limb, and our customary human world, where things of value are mostly people and things we have built or can extract? As an awkward boy in a complicated family leading the itinerant military life, this place kicked off a lifelong pursuit of that question. The green world, ancient and usually resilient, might have some lessons to teach. This website offers a place for me to work out some of that.

There have been many forests for me since then — in the Blue Ridge Mountains of Virginia, and on old dirt roads through the mountains of West Virgina. And for the last thirty years, a small forest in Seattle, on a peninsula, in a city park. It is old-growth, a full 500 years old, a rare remnant of the million acres of forest once found here in the Puget Lowlands, between the Cascade Mountains to the east, and the Olympic Mountains to the west. Giant Douglas-firs, tiny Douglas squirrels. Pileated woodpecker and crowned kinglets. Hemlock and Cedar. Eagle and owl.

This old forest is where I go — somtimes , as when I was a kid, on my bike. I do my best to take in its complex beauty, maybe to learn some of the secrets of the green world.

Some of that elegance, some of those secrets, are found below.

Light Seeking

Doug firs grow tall and mostly straight. Hemlocks and cedar mostly do too. That's the way of conifers. But in Seward's forest, look closely, and you can see that deciduous trees and shrubs send their branches out looking for light. They have a diverse family of light-sensing proteins, more than herbaceous plants and conifers; each protein is evolved to detect a different kind — a different frequency &mdahs; of light. Phytochromes, cryptochromes, phototropins. These sensing proteins allow the tree's decision-making apparatus to distinguish light in shade from light coming direct from the sun.

One of the consequences of this decision making is that — as you can see — neighboring trees and shrubs rarely collide. Instead they form elegant configurations of branch and leaf, arabesques of branches thick and thin, together maximizing the amount of sunlight energy captured after its long journey from the sun. In a monoculture of trees or plants, a doug-fir plantation or a corn field, this non-interfering light collection is less obvious. But in an old forest such as ours, with many species and many layers, and with centuries available to find an energy-maximizing collective configuration, the most beautiful branching patterns emerge.

Kenneth Rexroth: Identity with a beneficient environment

Rexroth (1905-1982) was poet, essayist, translator from Chinese and Japanese, self-educated, sometimes described as the father of the Beats (Burroughs, Ginsburg, Kerouac). In the essay "American Indian Songs", excerpted here, he claims a contrast between indigenous and western self understanding: identity with, versus separation from, the natural world.

It might be that the difference is tied to, is partly correlated with where one spends one's time — in a building, in front of a screen, or in a wild(-ish) forest.

Western European man characteristically regards himself not only as an independent entity in a fundamentally hostile environment but as the relatively permanent factor in a perishing world and the sole source in it of value. Most Western European poetry is, even in its erotic lyric "Gather ye rosebuds while ye may" concerned with the tragedy of the waste of value in a world of fact.

There is nothing of this in American Indian poetry. The intense aesthetic realization which precedes the poem is a realization of identity with a beneficent environment. Often this is focused in a dream or vision, waking or sleeping, after long lonely fast and vigil in the forest or desert. An aspect of the environment, an animal or a natural object or force, appears to the Indian, waiting in a trance state, and gives him the song, which remains his most precious possession and the pivot of his life forever after. As such, however simple, these songs always express mutual acceptance and approval of the self and the other, focused but also generalized, amounting to identification.

In other words, the holy is not the Judeo-Protestant "utterly other" a term of Otto's but the utterly same. They also express the accompanying emotional state - a feeling of extraordinarily intense hyperesthesia, concentration of all faculties in one realization, and the emotional tone of the realization itself - what we call transfiguration and transcendence - the kind of general sacramentalism we identify with St. Francis's Canticles and certain poems of Wordsworth or Francis James. (from David Hinton, The Wilds of Poetry, p76, quoted here.

Essay is from Rexroth's "Assays", excerpted at some length in a blogpost by Jerome Rothenberg.

The Oxygen-Evolving Complex (OEC): "most precious of all jewels"

So suggests evolutionary biochemist Nick Lane in his 2009 book Life Ascending: The Ten Great Inventions of Evolution:

The oxygen-evolving complex is the nutcracker that pinions water ‘just so’ to have its electrons cracked out one by one. When all the electrons are removed, the invaluable waste, oxygen, is flushed out into the world. The oxygen-evolving complex is really a component of Photosystem II, but sits at the very edge, facing the outside world, and gives a sense of being ‘tacked on’. It’s shockingly small. The complex is a cluster of four manganese atoms and a single calcium atom, all held together by a lattice of oxygen atoms. And that’s that.

For some years, the irrepressible Mike Russell, whom we already met in Chapters 1 and 2, has argued that the structure of this complex is remarkably similar to some minerals cooked up in hydrothermal vents, such as hollandite or tunnel calcium manganite. But until 2006, we didn’t know the structure of the manganese cluster in atomic resolution, and Russell’s was a voice in the wilderness. But now we know. And although Russell was not quite right, his broad conception was absolutely correct. The structure, as revealed by a team headed by Vittal Yachandra at Berkeley, bears a striking resemblance to what he proposed:

"ancient mineral structure ... four manganese atoms linked by oxygen in a lattice, with a calcium atom nearby"
Whether the original oxygen-evolving complex was simply a bit of mineral that got wedged in Photosystem II, we don’t know. Perhaps manganese atoms became bound to oxygen in a lattice as they were oxidised by ultraviolet radiation, seeding the growth of a tiny crystal on site. Perhaps the proximity of this cluster to chlorophyll, or to adjacent bits of protein, distorted it a little in some way, optimising its function. But whatever the origin of the cluster, there is a huge sense of the accidental about it. It is far too close to a mineral structure to be the product of biology. Like a few other metal clusters found at the heart of enzymes, it is almost certainly a throwback to the conditions found billions of years ago in a hydrothermal vent.

Most precious of all jewels, the metal cluster was wrapped in a protein and held in trust for all eternity by the cyanobacteria. However it formed, this little cluster of manganese atoms opened up a new world, not only for the bacteria that first trapped it, but for all life on our planet. Once it formed, this little cluster of atoms started to split water, the four oxidised manganese atoms combining their natural avidity to yank electrons from water, thereby releasing oxygen as waste. Stimulated by the steady oxidation of manganese by ultraviolet radiation, the splitting of water would have been slow at first. But as soon as the cluster became coupled to chlorophyll, electrons would have started to flow. Getting faster as chlorophyll became adapted to its task, water was sucked in, split open, its electrons drawn out, oxygen discarded. Once a trickle, ultimately a flood, this life-giving flow of electrons from water is behind all the exuberance of life on earth. We must thank it twice–once for being the ultimate source of all our food, and then again for all the oxygen we need to burn up that food to stay alive.

It’s also the key to the world’s energy crisis. We have no need for two photosystems, for we’re not interested in making organic matter. We only need the two products released from water: oxygen and hydrogen. Reacting them together again generates all the energy we’ll ever need, and the only waste is water. In other words, with this little manganese cluster, we can use the sun’s energy to split water, and then react the products back together again to regenerate water–the hydrogen economy. No more pollution, no more fossil fuels, no more carbon footprints, no more anthropogenic global warming, albeit still some danger of explosions. If this little cluster of atoms changed the makeup of the world long ago, knowing its structure should be the first step to changing our own world today. As I write, chemists around the world are racing to synthesise this tiny manganese cluster in the lab, or something similar that works as well. Soon, surely, they will succeed. And then it can’t be long before we learn to live on water and a splash of sunshine.

Big Trees, Burned Bark

Douglas-fir trees start out as tiny seedlings on bare ground beneath an open sky. They live for centuries, and rarely reproduce beneath the closed canopy. That is their strategy: start after fire or severe windstorms, grow tall, acquire thick bark, live long, reseed only when the next calamity strikes.

Research suggests that massive fires swept through the Puget Lowlands early in the sixteenth century, with a stand-replacement fire striking here in 1508, at what we now call the Seward Park peninsula — perhaps then known by its Lushootseed name sbəqʷábs ("it has a fat nose").

The seedling in the video shows how our big firs looked just one year after that fire. In the concluding drone shot, our tree is at the center with a broken top, with other big firs all around.

Another regional fire – this time not quite so severe – followed two hundred years later, in 1701. Our “class of 1500” firs had by then acquired thick, fire-resistant, deeply furrowed bark. This second fire left the burn marks which we see today. A hundred firs and a few cedars survived. And perhaps many sword ferns, which havea life history similar to Douglas-firs and have woody rhizomes tucked away safely underground.
