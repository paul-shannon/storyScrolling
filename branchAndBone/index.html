Branch and Bone

Lessons from a small forest

I encountered my first forest at age seven while riding my bike on the military base where I then lived. I came upon a sun-dappled stream running beneath a low bridge. There were no other people around, only tall trees, understory and the stream. Of course I had seen trees before, and surely a river or two. But I had never seen such an entire interwoven green world (as it then seemed to me) — growing on its own terms with such beauty. A place where I felt at home.

That early encounter posed a question I have been studying on ever since.

What is the relationship between this green world, of sunlight and water, of leaf and limb, and our customary human world, of straight lines, right angles, filled almost entirely of things built and extracted, things we choose, place and whose fate we determine.?
I was an awkward boy from a complicated family, a family which led the itinerant military life, with few social connections, no extended family, no cousins, aunts or uncles. This place, and the contrast it offered, drew me in, kicked off my lifelong pursuit of that question. I figured that this green world, ancient and usually resilient, beauty untouched by human hands or thought, might have some lessons to teach.

This website offers a place for me to work out some of that.

There have been many forests for me since then — decades walking in the Blue Ridge Mountains of Virginia and on old dirt roads through the mountains of West Virgina then, for the last thirty years, a small forest in Seattle, on a peninsula, in a city park.

This is old-growth, a full 500 years since the last stand-replacement fire, a rare remnant of the million acres of forest once found here in the Puget Lowlands, between the Cascade Mountains to the east, and the Olympic Mountains to the west.

Giant Douglas-firs, tiny Douglas squirrels, pileated woodpeckers and crowned kinglets, hemlock and cedar, eagle and owl.

This old forest is where I go — somtimes, as when I was a kid, by bike. I do my best to take in its complex beauty, try to learn some of the secrets of its green world.

Some of that elegance and some of those secrets are described below. A mix of disciplines and strategies help out: natural history, molecular biology, geology, forest ecology, fire history. But not least: just looking. On a rare day walking here, with all those strategies engaged, the analytic aspects fall away and I find myself but another "true creature of no rank" of this old endlessly creative planet, my bones not so different from tree branches, my breathing interleaved with and depending upon sunlight capture by leaves, my cognition just another version of all the sensing and deciding going on all around me in the other creatures of this green world, all of us made from some combination of these things:

sunlight and water
leaf and lung
branch and bone

Light Seeking

Doug firs, hemlocks and cedars grow tall and straight. That's the way of conifers. Look closely in Seward's forest, and you will see that deciduous trees and shrubs send their branches far out from their trunk looking for light. Especially bigleaf maple, the beaked hazelnut shrub, and madrone: their branches are often flat out launched.

The deciduous woody plants have an especially diverse family of light-sensing proteins, more than herbaceous plants and conifers. Each protein has evolved to detect a different a different frequency of light. Phytochromes, cryptochromes, phototropins, among others. These sensing proteins allow the tree's decision-making apparatus to make fine distinctions. Especially useful is the ability to distinguish red light (direct from the sun) from far-red light (filtered through leaves). Detecting this, the plant then summons resources and signalling hormones. Branches reach for sun — as if with a non-agression pact.

One of the consequences of this decision making is that neighboring trees and shrubs rarely collide. Instead they form elegant configurations of branch and leaf, arabesques of branches thick and thin, together maximizing the amount of sunlight energy captured after its long journey from the sun. In a monoculture of trees or plants, a doug-fir plantation or a corn field, these non-interfering light collection strategies are less obvious. But in an old forest such as ours, with many species and many layers, and with centuries available to find an energy-maximizing collective configuration, the most beautiful branching patterns emerge.

The Indispensable Molecule: Photosystem II

From the PDB, Molecule of the Month (2004)
Photosystem II captures the energy from sunlight and uses it to extract electrons from water molecules Three billion years ago, our world changed completely. Before then, life on Earth relied on the limited natural resources found in the local environment, such as the organic molecules made by lightning, hot springs, and other geochemical sources. However, these resources were rapidly being used up. Everything changed when these tiny cells discovered a way to capture light and use it to power their internal processes. The discovery of photosynthesis opened up vast new possibilities for growth and expansion, and life on the earth boomed. With this new discovery, cells could take carbon dioxide out of the air and combine it with water to create the raw materials and energy needed for growth. Today, photosynthesis is the foundation of life on Earth, providing (with a few exotic exceptions) the food and energy that keeps every organism alive.

The Oxygen-Evolving Complex (OEC): "most precious of all jewels"

So suggests evolutionary biochemist Nick Lane in his 2009 book Life Ascending: The Ten Great Inventions of Evolution:

The oxygen-evolving complex is the nutcracker that pinions water ‘just so’ to have its electrons cracked out one by one. When all the electrons are removed, the invaluable waste, oxygen, is flushed out into the world. The oxygen-evolving complex is really a component of Photosystem II, but sits at the very edge, facing the outside world, and gives a sense of being ‘tacked on’. It’s shockingly small. The complex is a cluster of four manganese atoms and a single calcium atom, all held together by a lattice of oxygen atoms. And that’s that.

For some years, the irrepressible Mike Russell, whom we already met in Chapters 1 and 2, has argued that the structure of this complex is remarkably similar to some minerals cooked up in hydrothermal vents, such as hollandite or tunnel calcium manganite. But until 2006, we didn’t know the structure of the manganese cluster in atomic resolution, and Russell’s was a voice in the wilderness. But now we know. And although Russell was not quite right, his broad conception was absolutely correct. The structure, as revealed by a team headed by Vittal Yachandra at Berkeley, bears a striking resemblance to what he proposed:

"ancient mineral structure ... four manganese atoms linked by oxygen in a lattice, with a calcium atom nearby"
Whether the original oxygen-evolving complex was simply a bit of mineral that got wedged in Photosystem II, we don’t know. Perhaps manganese atoms became bound to oxygen in a lattice as they were oxidised by ultraviolet radiation, seeding the growth of a tiny crystal on site. Perhaps the proximity of this cluster to chlorophyll, or to adjacent bits of protein, distorted it a little in some way, optimising its function. But whatever the origin of the cluster, there is a huge sense of the accidental about it. It is far too close to a mineral structure to be the product of biology. Like a few other metal clusters found at the heart of enzymes, it is almost certainly a throwback to the conditions found billions of years ago in a hydrothermal vent.

Most precious of all jewels, the metal cluster was wrapped in a protein and held in trust for all eternity by the cyanobacteria. However it formed, this little cluster of manganese atoms opened up a new world, not only for the bacteria that first trapped it, but for all life on our planet. Once it formed, this little cluster of atoms started to split water, the four oxidised manganese atoms combining their natural avidity to yank electrons from water, thereby releasing oxygen as waste. Stimulated by the steady oxidation of manganese by ultraviolet radiation, the splitting of water would have been slow at first. But as soon as the cluster became coupled to chlorophyll, electrons would have started to flow. Getting faster as chlorophyll became adapted to its task, water was sucked in, split open, its electrons drawn out, oxygen discarded. Once a trickle, ultimately a flood, this life-giving flow of electrons from water is behind all the exuberance of life on earth. We must thank it twice–once for being the ultimate source of all our food, and then again for all the oxygen we need to burn up that food to stay alive.

Big Trees, Burned Bark

Douglas-fir trees start out as tiny seedlings on bare ground beneath an open sky. They live for centuries, and rarely reproduce beneath the closed canopy. That is their strategy: start after fire or severe windstorms, grow tall, acquire thick bark, live long, reseed only when the next calamity strikes.

Research suggests that massive fires swept through the Puget Lowlands early in the sixteenth century, with a stand-replacement fire striking here in 1508, at what we now call the Seward Park peninsula — perhaps then known by its Lushootseed name sbəqʷábs ("it has a fat nose").

The seedling in the video shows how our big firs looked just one year after that fire. In the concluding drone shot, our tree is at the center with a broken top, with other big firs all around.

Another regional fire – this time not quite so severe – followed two hundred years later, in 1701. Our “class of 1500” firs had by then acquired thick, fire-resistant, deeply furrowed bark. This second fire left the burn marks which we see today. A hundred firs and a few cedars survived. And perhaps many sword ferns, which havea life history similar to Douglas-firs and have woody rhizomes tucked away safely underground.

Aggressive English Ivy Takes Over

Seward's forest in approximately its current form has been on the peninsula for about 6000 years. The fire return interval (FRI) - for stand replacement fires - is about 500 years. So this forest probably burned to the ground twelve times. But with no permanent effect: even such catastrophic events as these are routinely recovered from.

But even this reslient forest can meet its match.

English Ivy, introduced to Washington state in 1889, is quite another story. It is a cunning interloper for which the forest community has no defense, and no response. Ivy flourishes on the ground, on tree trunks, and in tree canopies. It grows year round. It is toxic. It is hugely destructive.

If left to grow unchecked English Ivy would destroy the forest. Given enough time - perhaps 10,000 years? - other species would arrive or evolve; a new balance might be struck, perhaps the forest would return.

So a sustained community effort was organized to remove large amounts of ivy in 2004. Then Seattle Parks contractors and a few of us volunteers, just a few years ago, cleared the area in this photo. We check back regularly to see if English Ivy, Himalayan Blackberry or English Holly has found a foothold.
