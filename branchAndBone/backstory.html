Felicitous Falsehoods

My goal here is to shed light on the development in the west of pervasive notions of human exceptionalism. My title "branch and bone" suggests the opposite, that tree creatures and human creatures are significantly and substantially similar. Which is not to say that logging and genocide are equivalent acts. But it is to say that learning to see the continuity between us, and among all life forms, is overdue.

The development of pervasive cultural beliefs is hard to trace. Counter-examples will be easy to fine for any claim I make or any pattern I identify.

So let me set some ground rules, beginning with a distinction philosopher Catherine Elgin makes between knowledge and understanding. "Knowledge" consists of verifiable facts and invariant (physical) laws. Whereas "understanding" is contextual, interpretive, usually taking the form of a network of plausible beliefs.

This distinction shows up in Elgin's proposal that the epistemic success of science is derived as much from understanding as from knowledge. Another philosopher of science, Nancy Cartwright, suggests that knowledge emerges from understanding only via well-engineered settings in which many extraneous circumstances have been rigorously excluded. Stanford's linear accelerator is one example. Galileo's inclined plane is another.

Elgin illuminates this distinction, and illuminates how science actually works: science unabashedly relies on models, idealizations, and thought experiments that are known not to be true ... [yet] modern science is one of humanity's greatest cognitive achivements. To think that this achievement is a fluke would be mad.

We should not cavalierly assume that the inaccuracy of models and idealizations constitutes an inadequacy; quite the opposite. I suggest that their divergence from truth or representational accuracy fosters their epistemic functioning.

When effective, models and idealizations are, I contend, felicitous falsehoods... Although [they] are inaccurate - sometimes wildly inaccurate - they exemplify features they share with their targets and thereby afford epistemic access to aspects of their targets that are otherwise overshadowed or underemphasizes. They are true enough. [Catherine Elgin, 2017, p1].

The Nixon cartoon illustrates all this. I proceed next to Rene Descartes, a foundational thinker of the modern era, lamentably boring to read, but - it is my hope - easy and important to understanding when I reduce him to a true enough, felicitously false caricature, and thereby shed some light on how we moderns tend to overlook out shared identity with the other creatures on earth.
