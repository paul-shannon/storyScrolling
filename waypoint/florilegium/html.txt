  <h3> Wild Thought</h3>
"Wild thought" and not "the thought of wild men." For this book
departs from traditional ethnology in taking as its theme a universal
aspect of the human mind: thought in its wild state, flourishing in
every human mind - contemporary or ancient, near or far - insofar as it
has
  <blockquote><i><b>not been cultivated and domesticated to increase its
    yield.</i></b>
  </blockquote>

<p>  
Certainly, one can seek examples of wild thought among societies
without writing and without machines; even here, this thought
singularly resembles that which we find at work around ourselves, in
poetry and art or, again, in the diverse forms of popular lore,
whether archaic or recent.

<p>
<p>
In wild thought, there is nothing
disorderly or confused. Starting from an observation of the world that
bears witness to an often stupefying sense of detail and precision, it
analyzes, differentiates, classes, combines, and opposes... In this
book, consequently, myths, rituals, beliefs, and other cultural facts
appear as "wild" beings comparable, beyond language, to all those that
nature (from which we cannot separate the human mind) also gives birth
to in innumerable animal, plant, and mineral forms. It should not
surprise us, then, that over millennia of cohabitation, wild thought
should have found in these forms the material and inspiration for a
logic whose laws are limited to transposing the properties of the
real, and that for this very reason permitted men to grasp it.

  <h4>Kenneth Rexroth: Identity with a beneficient environment</h4>

  
  Western European man characteristically regards himself not only as an
  independent entity in a fundamentally hostile environment but as the
  relatively permanent factor in a perishing world and the sole source in it of
  value. Most Western European poetry is, even in its erotic lyric "Gather ye
  rosebuds while ye may" concerned with the tragedy of the waste of value in a
  world of fact.

  <p>There is nothing of this in American Indian poetry. The intense
  aesthetic realization which precedes the poem is a realization of identity
  with a beneficent environment. Often this is focused in a dream or vision,
  waking or sleeping, after long lonely fast and vigil in the forest or
  desert. An aspect of the environment, an animal or a natural object or force,
  appears to the Indian, waiting in a trance state, and gives him the song,
  which remains his most precious possession and the pivot of his life forever
  after. As such, however simple, these songs always express mutual acceptance
  and approval of the self and the other, focused but also generalized,
    amounting to identification.

    <p>In other words, the holy is not the
  Judeo-Protestant "utterly other" a term of Otto's but the utterly same. They
  also express the accompanying emotional state - a feeling of extraordinarily
  intense hyperesthesia, concentration of all faculties in one realization, and
  the emotional tone of the realization itself - what we call transfiguration
  and transcendence - the kind of general sacramentalism we identify with
  St. Francis's Canticles and certain poems of Wordsworth or Francis James.

      (from David Hinton, The Wilds of Poetry, p76, quoted
      <a href="https://www.bopsecrets.org/rexroth/indiansongs.htm"
         target="_blank">here</a>.

    <p> Essay is from Rexroth's "Assays", excerpted at some length
      in a
      <a href="https://poemsandpoetics.blogspot.com/2009/10/kenneth-resroth-american-indian-songs.html"
      target="_blank">blogpost</a> by Jerome Rothenberg.

<h3>Thinking with an old-growth forest</h3>

<div style="margin: 100px; margin-top: 20px; margin-bottom: 0">
<center>
  <video controls preload muted>
    <source src="https://sewardpark.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/bigTree-7.mov">
  </video>
</center>
</div>
<p>

  We humans tend to think with, and to use metaphors inspired by the
  compelling items in our environment, especially when novel -
  surprising - and   powerful.  They excite analogies which sometimes then
  diffuse through the culture, picked up as explanatory
  strategies in other domains.  Our imaginations
  are shaped by the compelling objects, forces, processes that surround us.  This is, to
  adopt the term, the <b>imaginary</b> in which we live.  It is various, it is historical,
  yet trends over time can be seen. 

  <p>
  Trees and forests in urban life are rarely surprising, powerful or
  compelling.
  <p>
  But Seward Park's urban old-growth forest can be all three.

  <p> Here are a few examples of this phenomenon.  I suggest we
  should add this old-growth forest to the list.

  <ul>
    <li> Aristotle:  his close study of the animals in bay of Kallonis on the Greek
      island of Lesbos, shaped the "biological" metaphysics for which he is known.
    <li> Darwin:  Lyell's uniformitarianism, the lands visited during his
      five years sailing on the Beagle, Malthus on population all influenced his evolutionary ideas.
    <li> Freud:  the energy flows and regulation in the steam engine,
    along with 19th century advances in hydraulics, offered models for the human mind.
    <li> Cogntive psychology: brains were modeled after computers.
   </ul>
<p>
In the last 500 years, with Luther's and Calvin's Reformation, the
rise of early capitalism, the nation state, and the growing presence
of machines in our lives, we have new imaginaries to think with.

<p>

Especially for urban citizens of techological cultures, who live in
an almost entirely human-built world, with straight lines everywhere - the
carpentered environment - often, too often perhaps,  where the primary
forces and agents of change are human.   One common imaginary
of the present day is organized around buying and selling, humans as
customers, everything a commodity best understood in terms of its
value in the market.  Something special is attributed to humans; all
other species are ancillary, surbordinate, of only passing interest.

<p> But just down the hill, a fifteen minute walk, lies quite a different "compelling
item", one that draws my attention, and which I use to think with. It
provides metaphors which guide me, models to reason with: 120 acres of
Douglas-Fir old-growth forest.  In what follows I explore this forest
and I explore the imaginary it has shaped in me.
